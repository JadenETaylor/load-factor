The AI boom is an electricity story.

Load Factor is a research notebook on the economics of new sectors. Chips need data centers, data centers need power, and power needs reactors and batteries. Follow the chain.

AI infrastructureAbout $730B of capital spending guided for 2026 by four companies
Electricity demandData center load set to more than double by 2030
NuclearFirm power, slow and costly to buildGrid storageFast to build, returns already under pressure
Further out, with their own logic:Quantum computingSpace economy

The argument in one page

The largest investment cycle in the economy right now starts with a handful of companies buying chips. Four of them have guided to roughly $730 billion of capital spending in 2026 alone. [2] That spending turns into data centers, and data centers turn into electricity demand. The IEA expects data centers to account for nearly half of US electricity demand growth through 2030. [3]

So the question an economist should ask about AI is partly a question about power. Who can supply it, how fast, and at what cost? Nuclear offers steady output but new plants are the most expensive source of power Lazard tracks. [4] Batteries are quick to build, but in Texas the average battery earned 84% less in 2025 than at the 2023 peak. [5] Each link in the chain has a different bottleneck and a different way to lose money.

Quantum and space sit outside the chain. They are here because they raise the two measurement problems that show up everywhere in new sectors: how to value something that has no revenue yet, and how to trust a market size when nobody agrees what is in the market.

AI infrastructure: the spending is certain, the return is not

Five companies are on track to roughly double their capital spending in a single year. This page looks at what that money has to earn.

The short version

  • Spending by the largest buyers roughly doubled in a year and now exceeds the cash their businesses generate.
  • Whether it pays depends on two things nobody can observe yet: how long the chips stay useful and how much revenue each one earns.
  • The first signs of moderation appeared in the October 2026 earnings reports.

What the numbers say

Company2026 capital spending guidanceDetail from the latest quarter
AmazonAbout $220BHeld guidance. Free cash flow was negative $42.4B. [2]
Alphabet$195B to $205BMaintained guidance. [2]
MicrosoftAbout $170BLowered from $175B. [2]
Meta$135B to $145BNarrowed from $130B to $145B. [3]

Taking the midpoint of each range gives about $730 billion for these four companies. Two details matter more than the total. First, Amazon reported negative free cash flow, which means the spending is no longer funded entirely by operations. S&P expects all six of the largest hyperscalers to show negative free operating cash flow in 2026 and 2027. [12] Second, CNBC described signs that the pace is moderating, with Microsoft trimming its number. [2]

Not all of the increase buys more capacity. Microsoft attributed $25 billion of its plan to higher component prices. [13] When input prices rise, nominal spending overstates real investment. That is the same adjustment you would make to GDP.

The economics: a capital cycle with a short fuse

A capital cycle works like this. High expected returns attract investment. Investment adds capacity. Capacity eventually outruns demand, prices fall, and returns disappoint. What makes this cycle unusual is how quickly the assets wear out in economic terms.

The depreciation question

Most large buyers spread the cost of servers over about six years. [7] Critics argue chips are replaced by better ones every two to three years, so the true life is shorter. The stakes are large. On roughly $425 billion of 2026 server purchases, a three year life means $142 billion a year in depreciation. A six year life means $71 billion. [8] One estimate puts the cumulative effect on reported earnings above $176 billion for 2026 through 2028 if the shorter life is right. [9]

The defense is that older chips move down to less demanding work and keep earning. Both sides can be right about the hardware and still disagree about the money, because a chip earns most of its revenue early. Amazon has already shortened the assumed life of some servers from six years to five. [7]

The revenue side

Revenue is growing very fast from a small base. OpenAI and Anthropic together went from about $30 billion in annualized revenue at the start of 2026 to $105 billion by late summer. [5] Read that figure with care. Annualized revenue multiplies a recent month by twelve, and the two companies count sales through cloud partners differently, so their numbers are not directly comparable. [6]

Some projections put total hyperscaler AI investment above $5 trillion over the next four years, which would require revenue in the trillions. [4]

Model: what does one year of spending have to earn?

This model asks a narrow question. If a company spends a given amount on equipment this year, how much new revenue does that equipment need to bring in each year to cover its own depreciation and a return for investors?

revenue needed per year

Revenue = spending × (1 ÷ life + required return) ÷ gross margin. This treats all spending as equipment with one life. In practice about a third goes to buildings and land that last far longer, so treat the result as an upper range.

Try the two presets. Moving from a six year life to a three year life raises the revenue needed by more than half. That single assumption matters more than anything else in the model.

Where the chain continues: electricity

The IEA projects global data center electricity use of about 945 TWh in 2030, up from roughly 415 TWh in 2024. [10] [11] Globally that is only around a tenth of demand growth. In the United States it is nearly half. [10] The gap between those two shares is the reason American power markets, and not global ones, are where the strain shows first.

This is where the story leaves software. Read on for the two technologies competing to supply that power: nuclear and grid storage.

  • Free cash flow at the largest buyers. Negative numbers shift the funding question to debt markets.
  • Any further changes to assumed server lives in annual reports.
  • Rental prices for older chips, which reveal how much later year revenue really exists.

Nuclear: demand finally showed up, delivery is the hard part

Tech companies want steady power and will sign long contracts for it. This page looks at why the cheapest nuclear is the kind that is already built.

The short version

  • New nuclear is the most expensive source of power Lazard tracks, yet tech companies are signing contracts for it because it runs around the clock.
  • Restarting a closed plant costs a fraction of building one, which is why restarts come first.
  • For new builds, the cost of capital and the length of construction matter more than the reactor itself.

What the numbers say

DealSizeTypeTiming

Google and Kairos Power500 MWNew small reactorsBy 2035 [7]

One tracker counts 13 announced deals totaling more than 9.8 GW. [5] Spending on new projects is estimated at about $20 billion in 2026, rising toward $50 billion by 2029. [8] The IEA has identified plans for up to 25 GW of small modular reactors and expects the first to come online around 2030. [10] [11]

The economics: nuclear is a financing problem

A nuclear plant is almost all upfront cost. Fuel and operations are cheap once it runs. That makes the price of its power extremely sensitive to two things that have little to do with physics: how long construction takes and what interest rate the money costs.

What Vogtle taught

The two new reactors at Vogtle in Georgia came online seven years late at about $35 billion, more than double the original $14 billion estimate. [3] Unit 3 took 14 years from groundbreaking to operation. [9] An MIT assessment put the overnight cost at $7,956 per kW and blamed an incomplete design at the start, management turnover, and first of a kind problems. The same study argues the next unit should cost about $4,300 per kW and the tenth about $2,900. [4]

That gap between the first unit and the tenth is a learning curve. The trouble is that someone has to pay for units one through nine. In Georgia, regulators estimated a typical household would pay about $14 more a month. [13]

Why restarts come first

The Crane restart is budgeted at about $1.6 billion for 835 MW. [5] That is roughly $1,900 per kW, about a quarter of what Vogtle cost per kW before financing. The plant already exists, so the construction clock is short. Load the restart preset in the model below and compare it with a new build.

The small reactor bet

Small modular reactors try to fix the financing problem by being built faster in a factory. The MIT study estimates their cost per kW at 1.4 to 1.75 times the next large reactor because they give up economies of scale. [4] So the bet is that speed and repetition outweigh size. There is no commercial evidence yet either way. Holtec, with $400 million of federal support for a plant in Michigan, is competing with 30 other US developers to be first. [12]

Model: the levelized cost of a reactor

per MWh, levelized

Overnight cost is grown at the cost of capital for half the construction period to approximate interest during construction. That total is turned into an annual payment with a capital recovery factor and divided by annual output. The operations and fuel figure is my assumption, not a sourced number.

Hold everything fixed and move only the cost of capital from 8% to 5%. The price of power falls by roughly a third. This is why federal loans and long contracts with strong buyers matter so much. They do not change the reactor. They change the interest rate.

What I'm watching

  • Whether Crane returns to service on its accelerated schedule. It is the first real test.
  • The first firm price, not target price, for a small reactor project.
  • Who holds overrun risk in each contract: the developer, the tech buyer, or utility customers.
  • Repeat orders for a single design, which is the only way down the learning curve.
  • Interest rates. For this sector they are as important as engineering.

Grid storage: record installations, shrinking returns

Batteries are the fastest way to add capacity to a grid. This page looks at why the market with the most of them is also the one where they earn the least.

The short version

  • The US set an installation record in the second quarter of 2026, and storage is the fastest thing anyone can add to a grid.
  • In Texas, the market with the most batteries, average revenue per battery fell 84% from its 2023 peak.
  • Both facts are true at once. That is what a competitive market with easy entry looks like.

What the numbers say

The economics: a business that erodes its own margin

How a battery makes money

A grid battery buys power when it is cheap and sells when it is expensive. It can also be paid to stand ready to stabilize the grid, a set of products called ancillary services. Early batteries in Texas earned most of their money from those services because few competitors could respond as fast.

Why revenue fell

The market for ancillary services is small and fixed in size. When enough batteries arrive to fill it, the price collapses. Modo Energy says there is likely already enough storage in Texas to handle the jobs batteries are paid for today. [5] The same logic applies to buying low and selling high. Every battery that charges at noon raises the noon price a little, and every battery that discharges at dusk lowers the dusk price. The fleet narrows the very gap it lives on.

By March 2026 the average Texas battery earned $1.88 per kW per month, against a trailing twelve month average of $2.43. [6] New rules on minimum charge levels add pressure on one hour systems, which helps explain why recent projects are at least two hours long. [12]

Two cost stories that seem to conflict

BloombergNEF reports that battery packs for stationary storage fell 45% in price in 2025 to $70 per kWh. [8] Lazard reports that the all in cost of a US storage project rose in 2026, reversing the prior decline, and points to tariffs on imported cells. [9] [10] Both are right. One is a global price for a component. The other is a US price for a finished project. The difference between them is trade policy.

Model: how long until a battery pays for itself?

simple payback

Payback = installed cost per kW ÷ (monthly revenue × 12 − operating cost). Installed cost and operating cost are my illustrative assumptions. The two Texas revenue figures are from Modo Energy. The contract preset is hypothetical. No discounting or degradation, so real paybacks are longer.

At recent Texas market revenue the payback runs past the life of the equipment. Installations continue anyway because many projects do not rely on market prices. They sign contracts with utilities or large customers who pay a fixed fee for the capacity. The record build and the revenue collapse are describing two different ways of getting paid.

Capital is arriving in record amounts for a technology whose main product is still ahead of it. This page looks at how to think about value when most of it sits in the future.

The short version

  • Venture funding, exits, and government money all hit records in the past eighteen months.
  • Real revenue is appearing, but a large share of the headline growth at the biggest public company came from buying a chip foundry.
  • Valuing the sector is an exercise in probability and patience, and small changes in either move the answer a lot.

The economics: pricing something that mostly does not exist yet

Read the growth rate twice

IonQ's third quarter revenue rose 336%. Of the $173.8 million total, $83.5 million came from SkyWater, a chip foundry it acquired during the year. [5] [6] That is 48% of the quarter. The quantum platform itself grew 127%, which is still fast. [6] But the two numbers tell different stories, and only one of them is about quantum computing demand.

This is not a criticism of the deal. Owning manufacturing may be the right strategy, and quantum hardware is increasingly a chip fabrication story. [9] It is a reminder to separate growth a company earned from growth it bought.

Why governments are investors now

Public funding has shifted from research grants toward purchases and equity stakes. [1] In economic terms, governments are treating quantum as a strategic good with benefits a private investor cannot capture, such as security. That support lowers the risk of the sector running out of money. It does not make any single company's technology work.

Model: what is a long shot worth today?

Suppose a company has some chance of reaching a large payoff some years from now, and is worth little otherwise. Its value today is the payoff, times the probability, discounted for the wait.

present value

Value = probability × payoff ÷ (1 + discount rate) raised to the number of years. Every input here is hypothetical. The model ignores dilution from future fundraising, which in practice reduces what today's shareholders keep.

Move the years slider and watch how fast value drains away. A delay of a few years can cut the answer by close to a third even if nothing else changes. That is why this sector trades on roadmap news. Investors are constantly updating two numbers they cannot observe.

The economics: a market size is a definition first

Where the boundary sits

Launch is the visible part of the industry and a small part of the money. One 2026 report puts the commercial share of the $626 billion total at 78%, with most value in what satellites do and in ground equipment. [5] The broadest forecasts go further and count industries that merely rely on satellites. The World Economic Forum figure of $1.8 trillion takes that wider view. [4]

None of these is wrong. They answer different questions. The narrow figure tells you how much revenue space companies collect. The wide figure tells you how much economic activity would be disrupted without space. An investor needs the first. A policymaker might want the second.

Concentration

SpaceX listed on June 12, 2026 at $135 a share, valuing it near $1.75 trillion after raising about $75 billion. [6] [7] Its 2025 revenue was about $18.7 billion, so the listing price was roughly 94 times revenue. [6] [9] The Starlink internet service produced $11.4 billion of that revenue at a 63% operating margin before depreciation, from 10.3 million subscribers. [7] The company as a whole lost $4.9 billion in 2025. [8]

Two things follow. First, the most valuable space company is mostly a telecom business. Second, part of what investors are paying for is not space at all. SpaceX merged with an AI company in February 2026, and that segment consumed most of group capital spending in early 2026. [6] [7] The chain on the overview page reaches even here.

Seven ideas the sector pages lean on

Each framework below comes from standard economics and finance. Each is tied to a specific number elsewhere on this site so you can see it at work.

1. The capital cycle

High returns attract investment. Investment adds supply. Supply eventually exceeds demand and returns fall. The cycle is easiest to see in industries where capacity takes years to build, because by the time it arrives the conditions that justified it may be gone.

Where it shows up on this site

AI infrastructure is in the investment phase. The twist is asset life. On about $425 billion of 2026 server purchases, annual depreciation is $71 billion at a six year life and $142 billion at three. [1] A short lived asset gives the cycle less time to work out in the investor's favor.

See the model on the AI page.

2. Levelized cost and the discount rate

Levelized cost spreads everything a power plant costs over everything it produces, in today's dollars. For plants that are mostly upfront cost, the interest rate used to do the spreading matters as much as the construction bill.

Where it shows up on this site

This is the whole nuclear story. A reactor and a gas plant can have similar lifetime costs on paper and very different risk, because the reactor's money is all spent before the first unit of power is sold.

See the model on the nuclear page and try changing only the cost of capital.

3. Learning curves

Many manufactured goods get cheaper by a steady percentage each time total production doubles. The effect is strongest for small, repeatable products and weakest for large, custom projects.

Where it shows up on this site

Battery packs fell from $1,474 per kWh in 2010 to $108 in 2025. [3] An MIT study expects the next large US reactor to cost $4,300 per kW and the tenth $2,900. [2] The battery curve is observed. The reactor curve is a forecast that depends on someone ordering ten.

Compare the storage and nuclear pages.

4. Free entry and vanishing margins

When a business is profitable and easy to enter, competitors arrive until the extra profit is gone. The faster something can be built, the faster this happens.

Where it shows up on this site

Texas batteries are the textbook case. The average battery earned 84% less in 2025 than at the 2023 peak, largely because so many arrived. [4] Speed to build is the sector's advantage and also the reason its returns fall quickly.

See the payback model on the storage page.

5. Option value

Some investments are worth owning mainly because of what they might become. Their value is a probability times a payoff, discounted for time. Small changes in the probability or the wait produce large changes in value.

Where it shows up on this site

Quantum computing is priced this way. So, to a degree, is every small reactor developer without a finished plant.

See the model on the quantum page.

6. Externalities: who pays and who benefits

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on someone outside the transaction. Markets tend to produce too much of what has unpriced costs and too little of what has unpriced benefits.

Where it shows up on this site

Several appear in these sectors. Nuclear and storage reduce emissions that have no price in most US markets, so their private returns understate their social value. In the other direction, when a utility builds for data center demand, existing customers can end up sharing the bill. In Georgia, regulators estimated the Vogtle reactors would add about $14 a month to a typical household bill. [5] In the United States, data centers account for nearly half of expected electricity demand growth to 2030, [6] so the question of who pays for new grid capacity is going to come up often.

This lens matters for policy more than for stock prices, but policy is what sets the rules these investments live under.

7. Measurement comes before analysis

Before asking whether a number is good or bad, ask what it counts. Different definitions can produce different answers to what sounds like the same question.

Where it shows up on this site

Two trackers measured the same record quarter for US batteries and got 20.2 and 18.9 GWh. [8] [9] Space economy estimates for 2025 range from $439 billion to $626 billion. Two leading AI companies report revenue through partners in ways that are not comparable. [7]

The space page has a tool for checking growth claims against their own endpoints.

What these models leave out

  • Taxes, subsidies, and tax credits, which change real project returns materially.
  • Debt structure. Real projects mix debt and equity at different costs.
  • Degradation, outages, and maintenance schedules.
  • Correlation. A slowdown in AI spending would hit nuclear contracts and storage demand at the same time.
  • Prices that respond to the investment itself, which is the entire point of framework four.

The models are meant to build intuition about which inputs matter. They are not forecasts.
